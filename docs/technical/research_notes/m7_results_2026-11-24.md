**The trade-off (reported, not scored):**
- Each step up the frontier buys service with Lyft bookings: R0 → R1(15) → R2(15) → R3(2).
- **R2(15) and R1(10)** cost almost the same in bookings (8.8 against 7.6 per 100), but R2(15) misses 4.8 pp [3.8, 5.9] fewer riders.

- *Exploratory reading:* transferring by **predicted** wait (R2, R3) gets more for each booking than transferring by **elapsed** wait (R1). That matches the mechanism found in M2: an elapsed-time rule can send a rider to Lyft whose van was about to arrive.
- **Sensitivity (15 min):** the same order holds, with larger gaps. R3(2) − R0 is −11.9 pp [−13.8, −10.0].

## 3. R1u(10) never fires
- **What happened:** at both fleet sizes, R1u(10) made **0 transfers on all 30 nights**. Its runs are identical to R0's (the same outcomes file on r00).
- **Checks:** the manifests confirm the rule was `R1u(10)`.
- **Why:** R1u(10) fires only for a request **continuously without an assigned van** for 10 minutes. C places every waiting request into some van's plan within a few minutes, even when that van is far away. M2 recorded the same on 22 Oct.
- **What this means:** under an optimising dispatcher, "no van assigned" does not stand in for "waiting a long time". This is a finding about the rule, not a fault in the run.

**Rule means over 30 nights:**

| Rule | Not reached ≤ 20 min, % | ≤ 15 min, % | P90 passenger wait, min | Lyft bookings per 100 | Cancellations |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| R0 | 14.2 | 15.6 | 10.0 | 0.0 | 23.5 |
| R1(15) | 14.3 | 15.4 | 9.9 | 0.3 | 23.5 |
| R1(10) | 14.5 | 15.6 | 9.5 | 2.1 | 23.3 |
| R1u(10) | 14.2 | 15.6 | 10.0 | 0.0 | 23.5 |
| R2(15) | 13.3 | 13.5 | 9.6 | 2.2 | 22.0 |
| R3(2) | 12.4 | 12.8 | 9.2 | 3.4 | 20.8 |

- **Against R0:**
  - R3(2) misses 1.8 pp [1.0, 2.6] fewer riders and costs 3.4 more bookings per 100;
  - R2(15): 0.9 pp [0.2, 1.7] and 2.2;
  - the R1 rules are within the 2-pp margin of R0.
- **The frozen dominance output is circular here.** A pair of practically tied rules can each count as non-inferior to the other on all three measures and better on one. Under the rule as written, each then "dominates" the other. The pairs are:
  - R0 and R1(15) (R0 is better on bookings, R1(15) on P90 wait, by 0.0–0.3 min);
  - R1(15) and R1u(10) (the same, since R1u(10) = R0);
  - at δ_B = 2 only, R2(15) and R3(2).

  So the frozen nondominated list is empty. The code applies the rule exactly as preregistered; the gap is in the rule's wording, which never says what a mutual pair means.
- *Exploratory reading* (not preregistered; for the team, see §7): if a mutual pair is read as a tie, the frontier at 6 vans is:
  - δ_B = 2: R0 (= R1u(10)), R1(15), R3(2);
  - δ_B = 1: R0 (= R1u(10)), R1(15).

  In plain words, with six vans, R0 is as good as the transfer rules within the margins, except that R3(2) gains about 2 pp for about 3.4 bookings per 100.

## 6. Traced night (plan §6): r00, five vans, R0 against R2(15)
| | Not reached ≤ 20 min, % | P90 wait, min | Transfers | Cancellations | Van-km |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| R0 | 19.6 | 11.6 | 0 | 33 | 328 |
| R2(15) | 17.3 | 10.8 | 19 (20 riders) | 29 | 318 |

- **The 19 transfers:**
  - 8 rode in a Lyft, 1 was a Lyft no-show, and **10 cancelled after the transfer, before the Lyft arrived**;
  - 4 of those 10 would have cancelled under R0 as well, and the other 6 would have been picked up by a van under R0.
- **Across the grid** (exploratory, descriptive): 28–47% of transferred riders cancel before the Lyft arrives. The share is highest for R1(10) at five vans (47%), because elapsed-time rules transfer riders who have already waited long.
- **What drives this:** the assumed patience and the assumed 2-minute booking lag plus Lyft arrival time. Real Lyft wait data would move this number more than any other.
- **Paths diverge:** one transfer changes the vans' later plans. For example, R083 boarded a van in 3.6 min under R0 but was transferred at 10.2 min under R2(15). The traced night therefore shows the mechanism, not a rider-by-rider counterfactual.
