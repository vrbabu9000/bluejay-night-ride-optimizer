## 2 · One booking, several possible outcomes
**Purpose.** Give the vocabulary every later number depends on. [Script](speaking_script.md#s02)

**Definitions.**
- **Booking (request):** one request in the app, for 1–5 passengers.
- **Passenger:** one person.
- **Curb arrival:** a vehicle reaches the pickup point. Our service measure "reached within 15 min" uses this time.
- **Pickup:** the rider boards. Wait percentiles use the time a vehicle reached a rider who then boarded.
- **Cancellation:** the rider gives up before pickup. Under a transfer, the rider can cancel during the 2-min ordering lag or after the Lyft is ordered.
- **No-show:** the rider is absent when the vehicle arrives. A van waits up to 3 minutes (published policy).

**Assumptions shown on the tree.**
- In the pilot every offered transfer is accepted (consent 1.0, a simplification).
- "The dispatcher": today a person sends the Lyft (JHU page); in our model the dispatcher is the van optimizer plus the transfer rule.
- On the designed evening of slide 14, acceptance is 0.9.

**Why a tree.** Slides 18 and 19 are the two branches: how often each rule reaches riders, and what happens after the move to Lyft.

## 6 · How many requests should our scenario contain?
**Purpose.** Derive a per-van workload from aggregates. [Script](speaking_script.md#s06)

**Derivation.**
1. **Completed rides in the 6 pm hour.** C₁₈ ≈ 5,089 over 30 days (digitized; the tooltip reads 5,098), so per evening C₁₈ / 30 ≈ 170.
2. **Bookings per completed ride.** R/C = 50,297 / 24,095 = 2.087, so about 355 bookings in that hour.
3. **Load per vehicle.** ρ₁₈ = 170 × 2.087 ÷ 27.6 ≈ 12.85 bookings per vehicle per hour. The same formula gives ρ₁₉ = 10.10 and ρ₂₀ = 10.71.
4. **Five vans.** N_h = 5 ρ_h = 64.25 / 50.50 / 53.55. Rounding by largest remainder (total 168) gives **64 / 50 / 54** (D-35, D-36).

**The range.** About 150–260 per three-hour evening, illustrative, not a confidence interval (D-49).
- **Lower end:** if about 10% of bookings were rebookings, the count falls to about 151.
- **Upper end:** if Homewood had 20–35% fewer vehicles than "All services", it rises to 168 / 0.8 ≈ 210 up to 168 / 0.65 ≈ 258.

**Which levels ran.**
- **The pilot** uses 2 vans and 28 bookings in one hour, 40% of them in a 10-minute burst. That is 14 per van-hour against ρ₁₈ = 12.85: heavy by design.
- **The five-van evening** is designed but not run.

**Limitations.**
- Mixed-service vehicle counts.
- A 29-day vehicle average, which moves ρ by about ±3%.
- Unknown rebookings.

## 7 · Pickup and drop-off maps do not identify trips
**Purpose.** Show that place totals cannot identify pairs, so pairing is an assumption. [Script](speaking_script.md#s07)

**The toy example.** Two origins A and B each send 2 riders, and two destinations X and Y each receive 2.
- Table 1 (A→X 2, B→Y 2) and Table 2 (every pair 1) have identical row and column totals.
- Infinitely many tables do.

**Our method.**
1. **Gravity prior.** w_od = O_o · D_d · e^(−β t_od), with t_od the driving minutes. β = 0.1 per minute, so each minute multiplies the weight by about 0.905. Pairs under 60 s of driving are excluded.
2. **Iterative proportional fitting.** Rescale rows and columns alternately until both April totals are reproduced. The largest error is about 10⁻¹⁰.

**Why it is an assumption.** Every β reproduces the totals, so the data cannot choose β (D-18; design trail §5).

**Other assumptions.**
- Bookings share the geography of completed rides, because cancelled bookings' locations are unobserved.
- Arrival times are uniform within an hour.
- The geography is the same in every hour.

**Not yet tested.** A spread-out geography variant is built, but no experiment has used it.

**Figure.** The map shows the first synthetic pilot night, drawn by [make_map.py](make_map.py).

**Purpose.** Prevent reading "Canceled" as abandonment, and introduce patience. [Script](speaking_script.md#s09)

**Evidence.** 23,843 of 50,297 bookings (47.4%) ended "Canceled". The label likely mixes two events:

- **The riders' share** of "Canceled" is a sensitivity range of 50–75% (D-39, D-49).

- **Patience** is a hidden personal deadline drawn from a Weibull distribution with shape 1.5 and median 18 min.

## 10 · Why the no-show denominator matters
**Purpose.** Show that one count gives two rates. [Script](speaking_script.md#s10)

**Derivation.**
- Over all bookings: 2,359 / 50,297 = 4.7%.
- Per van arrival: 2,359 / (24,095 + 2,359) = 2,359 / 26,454 ≈ 8.9%.

**The condition.** The per-arrival rate holds only if all three are true:
- "Completed" means van-completed;
- "No Show" is recorded only after a real van arrival;
- Lyft hand-offs sit in "Canceled".

The arithmetic is exact; the uncertainty lies entirely in this status mapping (D-48). It is a *conditional derivation*, not a verified operating probability.

**Use.**
- p = 8.9% per arrival, provisional (D-40, D-48).
- There is one shared presence draw per rider, so the same rider is present or absent whichever vehicle arrives.
- Methodology Appendix C has the full argument.

## 12 · How the van optimizer chooses a plan
**Purpose.** Show the loop, keep its three parts distinct, and give one worked route choice. [Script](speaking_script.md#s12)

**The loop.** Epochs occur at every new booking, every cancellation or no-show of a booking in van planning, every van coming on or off duty, and every 2 minutes.
1. **Reveal and update.** The simulator reveals events and updates the observable state.
2. **Generate feasible routes.** For each van, every subset of at most 3 waiting bookings, each in its best stop order, with exact search. The van's committed work is always a candidate.
3. **Select compatible routes.** The MILP picks one route per van so that each waiting booking is served once or postponed.
4. **Evaluate the transfer rule.** The rule reads each waiting booking's predicted pickup: the plan's arrival time if the booking is planned, otherwise a tentative estimate computed outside the MILP.
5. **Apply accepted transfers.** They leave van planning at once.
6. **Re-plan once if needed.** The rule is not re-evaluated in the same second.
7. **Execute.** Vans depart; a van commits to a stop when it departs toward it.
8. **Repeat.**

Planning takes zero simulated time.

**The objective.** c = Σ q·φ(W) + 0.5 · Σ q·H + 0.1 · km. Here q is party size, W the wait in minutes and H the ride time including the 30-s boarding dwell.
- **φ** is piecewise linear, with slopes 1 / 2.5 / 5 / 10 / 20 points per passenger-minute after 0 / 5 / 10 / 15 / 20 minutes.
- So φ(5) = 5, φ(10) = 17.5, φ(15) = 42.5, φ(20) = 92.5. The 16th minute of waiting costs 10 points.
- Its convexity encodes the TransLoc example: φ(10) − φ(9) = 2.5 > φ(5) − φ(3) = 2.
- The ride weight 0.5 is below every slope of φ, so a waiting minute always outweighs a riding minute.

**The worked example** (illustrative; dwell omitted, as in methodology §6). One van; rider 1 (party of 1, waited 8 min) and a party of 2 (waited 1 min).

| Route | Waiting | Riding | Driving | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A, rider 1 first: W = 12, 8; H = 8, 9 | 27.5 + 2×12.5 = 52.5 | 0.5 × (8 + 18) = 13.0 | 1.0 | **66.5** |
| B, party first: W = 6, 16; H = 10, 5 | 2×7.5 + 52.5 = 67.5 | 0.5 × (20 + 5) = 12.5 | 1.0 | **81.0** |

A wins because in B rider 1's wait crosses 15 minutes.

**Postponement.** A booking left out of every chosen route is priced at π_r = b_r + M.
- b_r is the postponement price (slide 15).
- M is a per-epoch constant that makes every proven-optimal plan postpone the fewest bookings that the candidate set allows (backup B2). It does not guarantee that every booking is served.

**Constraints.**
- Hard: seats; pickup before drop-off.
- Guardrail, not a hard limit: ride time max(25 min, 2 × direct + 5 min). No candidate may create or worsen an excess, and existing excesses carry a penalty Ψ. A no-show wait between epochs can still push a ride over (slide 22).

**What "optimal" means.**
- The solver (HiGHS, relative gap 0) proves optimality over the candidates generated at that epoch. It is not optimal over all routes, nor over the evening.
- Points are an exchange rate between priorities (D-29), not money.
- **Three things kept distinct:** the simulator (grey), the route optimizer (blue) and the transfer rule (orange).

## 15 · Testing exposed an optimistic postponement price
**Purpose.** Show the scientific loop on our own model. [Script](speaking_script.md#s15)

**1 · Underpricing.** The first version charged a postponed booking b_r = q·φ(next tick − t_r), as if a van reached it at the next 2-minute tick.
- At 18:15 on the designed evening, a party of five that had waited 15 min was charged 262.5 points.
- The earliest any van could physically reach it was 18:19:28, worth 435.8 points.
- So postponing looked cheaper than any real service. The gap is largest for long waiters and large parties.

**2 · Controlled comparison.** Three formulations, each over the whole designed evening under vans only, each evolving from its own decisions (backup B5).

| | First version | Chosen | Route-based |
|---|---|---|---|
| Wait median / 90th pct / longest (min) | 17.1 / 31.0 / 44.4 | 18.6 / 31.2 / 35.6 | 21.1 / 34.3 / 38.6 |
| Mean wait (min) | 18.8 | 18.8 | 21.8 |
| Extra variables | 0 | 0 | up to 9,828 |

**3 · Simple approximation (chosen, provisional).** b_r = q·φ(max(next tick, L_r) − t_r), where L_r is the earliest time any on-duty van could reach the pickup.
- It is a lower bound on any future service, with three exceptions: a ≤ 13-s matrix inconsistency on 3 of 35,344 pairs, vans not yet on duty, and the next-tick floor.
- It adds no variables.
- It was chosen for its meaning and zero cost, not because one party was served sooner.

**4 · Remaining limitation.**
- The chosen price is still optimistic when the nearest van is busy, and it does not guarantee oldest-first service.
- On the designed evening it redistributed waiting: the youngest booking waited 35.6 min instead of 9.8.
- The route-based version can err in either direction, and in a synthetic five-van epoch it needed about 135,000 extra variables and 19.6 s against 3.0 s.

**Sources.** D-53; `outputs/sim/d53_experiment/d53_comparison.md`; independent review `research_notes/d53_pending_price_review_2026-10-22.md` (29 tests); math review 21 Oct §7 (the 18:15 example).

## 17 · What our verification establishes
**Purpose.** Separate verification from validation. [Script](speaking_script.md#s17)

**What each claim means.**
- **"To the second".** L1–L3 are designed evenings with every event time computed by hand. The simulator matches every van, curb arrival, pickup, drop-off, cancellation and no-show time exactly.
- **On L4** the Lyft decisions of the five transferring rules match the hand key: 22 transfers (4 of them XL) and 4 declined offers. These are totals over five separate rule runs.
- **"292,181".** An independent brute-force program enumerated every compatible combination of candidate routes at every epoch of two designed-evening runs: 151,710 under the first price and 140,471 under the chosen one. It found the same optimum and tie-break as the MILP every time.
- **"253 tests".** 46 test scenario generation and 207 test the simulator. 168 of the 207 were written by independent reviewers who did not reuse the simulator's logic.
- **"6 of 6".** 6 of 6 sampled reruns were byte-identical, and the 2 first-night batch runs equal the earlier pilot outputs. This is a sample, not every run.
- **Who wrote what.** The simulator and optimizer were written with an AI coding assistant under the team's direction (logged in `docs/ai_use_log.md`). The 168 independent tests and the brute-force checker were written by separate AI agents that did not reuse the simulator's code. "Independent" means separate derivation, not a different human team.

**Not validation.** Nothing here compares synthetic evenings with real nights or real riders.

**Purpose.** First finding, with its denominator explicit. [Script](speaking_script.md#s18)

| Rule | Reached ≤ 15 min (mean, SD) | Lyft orders / night |

| Vans only | 0.477 (0.095) | 0.00 |

**Reading the table.**

## 19 · A transfer does not end the rider's wait
**Purpose.** Second finding: what follows a transfer. [Script](speaking_script.md#s19)

**Fates of transferred bookings** (totals over 30 nights; each row reconciles).

| Rule | Transfers | Lyft completed | Lyft no-show | Cancelled in lag | Cancelled after booking |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Wait ten minutes | 196 | 94 | 7 | 18 | 77 |
| Wait fifteen minutes | 104 | 55 | 4 | 11 | 34 |
| Unassigned for ten minutes | 8 | 4 | 0 | 1 | 3 |
| Predicted total wait exceeds fifteen minutes | 294 | 187 | 14 | 14 | 79 |
| Lyft arrives sooner | 330 | 211 | 15 | 16 | 88 |

**The mechanism.** After a transfer the rider still waits:
- a 2-min ordering lag;
- then the Lyft delay (median 8 min);
- all while the hidden patience clock runs.

Under "Wait ten minutes", 95 of 196 cancelled before the Lyft arrived, 77 of them after the Lyft was ordered. The prediction rules lost about a third.

**Scope.**
- That a transfer starts a new wait, and so can lose riders, holds under any patience model.
- The *size* of the losses, and the *gap* between rules, depend on the placeholder. The Weibull shape 1.5 makes impatience grow with time waited, so a rider moved early has more patience left. Under constant-hazard (exponential) patience, the sensitivity shape kept in D-39, a 10-min Lyft wait would lose the same share whenever the transfer happened (about 32% at the same median, against about 41% vs 27% for riders who had waited 10 vs 1 min under the placeholder).
- The rules also differ in more than timing; no experiment isolated the cause.
- These are cancellations *after* transfer. Cancellations while waiting for a van are a separate count (backup B6).

## 20 · Choosing between service and resource use
**Purpose.** Introduce Pareto dominance and apply it descriptively. [Script](speaking_script.md#s20)

**Definitions.**
- **Service:** S = share of all bookings reached within 15 min (higher is better).
- **Lyft use:** B = Lyft orders per night (lower is better).
- **Dominance:** P dominates Q if S_P ≥ S_Q and B_P ≤ B_Q, with at least one strict.
- **Ties:** identical points do not dominate each other, so both are nondominated.

**On the 30-night means.**
- Five rules are nondominated.
- "Unassigned for ten minutes" is dominated by vans only: about the same service (0.476 vs 0.477) with slightly more Lyft use (0.23 vs 0 a night). Vans only would dominate it even with service exactly tied. It is identical to vans only on 24 nights; on the other 6 it reached more on 2 and fewer on 2 while ordering 7 Lyfts in all. No statistical margin is applied (D-50).
- The same five stay nondominated with van-km as a third objective.

| Rule | Nights nondominated |
|---|---|
| Vans only | 30 |
| Unassigned for ten minutes | 26 (inflated by 24 exact ties with vans only) |
| Predicted total wait exceeds fifteen minutes | 24 |
| Wait fifteen minutes | 14 |
| Lyft arrives sooner | 12 |
| Wait ten minutes | 11 |

**Paired with vans only.** The two prediction rules reach more on all 30 nights. "Wait ten minutes" reaches more on 19 and fewer on 6.

**Three trade-offs, kept separate.**
1. **Routing weights** inside the optimizer (slide 12): a fixed exchange rate, not a point on this chart.
2. **The policy trade-off** on this chart.
3. **The decision criteria** (D-50): not yet agreed, not applied.

**Supported and unsupported points.** Minimising "−S + λB" for any λ ≥ 0 picks only vans only, "Predicted total wait…" or "Lyft arrives sooner". The two waiting-time rules are nondominated but lie below the line from vans only to "Predicted total wait…", so no weighting selects them (check: the line gives 0.477 + 0.0276 B, i.e. 0.563 at B = 3.10 and 0.641 at B = 5.93, above their 0.495 and 0.514).

**Service per Lyft order.** Relative to vans only, (ΔS × 28) ÷ B is about 0.77 and 0.73 bookings reached per Lyft order for the prediction rules, and about 0.17 and 0.16 for "Wait ten / fifteen minutes".

**What the chart is not.**
- Nondominated is not recommended.
- Six tested rules are not a frontier: an untested rule or threshold could dominate them all.

## 21 · Could we optimize the transfer policy itself?
**Purpose.** Present leader–follower as a precise, unimplemented extension. [Script](speaking_script.md#s21)

**Concept.**
- A leader–follower (Stackelberg) model has a leader who chooses first, anticipating the follower's optimal response (von Stackelberg 1934).
- A bilevel program writes the follower's problem as a constraint of the leader's (Colson, Marcotte & Savard 2007, *Annals of OR* 153:235–256; Dempe 2002, *Foundations of Bilevel Programming*).

**One reading.**

  min over θ of E[ L(θ, y) + λ · B(θ) ]  subject to  y ∈ argmin over y′ ∈ Y(θ) of f(y′; θ)

- **Leader:** the policy designer choosing θ, for example a threshold.
- **Follower:** the route optimizer, re-planning for the bookings left after transfers.

**What we implement.** A sequential policy simulation: plan, rule, transfers, one re-plan. Across nights, six fixed rules are compared.

**Missing for a solved bilevel model.**
1. An upper-level optimization over θ.
2. The follower's optimal response stated as constraints over the evening. Ours optimizes one epoch at a time over restricted candidates.
3. The expectation over random nights.
4. An agreed λ.

**On simplifying the hierarchy.**
- Any single-level reduction needs specific assumptions, such as optimality conditions written as constraints or a value-function reformulation, each with its own requirements, notably for integer lower levels.
- We claim no such reduction, and aligned objectives do not by themselves collapse the hierarchy.
- Riders and Lyft are not optimizing players here.

## 22 · What we learned and what must be tested next
**Purpose.** Close honestly. [Script](speaking_script.md#s22)

**Lessons.**
- Assignment ≠ pickup.
- Transfer ≠ completion.
- Prediction-based rules changed both service and Lyft use.

**Limits.**
- Aggregate-only evidence.
- Placeholder patience.
- Presence that rests on a status mapping.
- A two-van pilot.
- At most 3 new pickups per van per epoch.
- One demand pattern.
- Verification, not validation.

**Guardrail excesses.**
- Plans never create one.
- Realized rides produced 36 excess events across the 180 runs: 16 distinct bookings on 10 nights, repeated across rules that made the same decisions. "36 rides" would overstate the count.
- Each coincided with a no-show wait on the same van while the rider was aboard, with an excess of at most 150 s per no-show plus a 13-s tolerance. This attribution is consistent with the timing; it is not a proof.

**Next, in order.**
1. Agree the decision criteria (D-50), then compute paired intervals on the saved nights. No new simulation is needed.
2. Sweep the thresholds.
3. Run the five-van evening and benchmark solve times (D-51).
4. Test sensitivity to patience and Lyft delay.
