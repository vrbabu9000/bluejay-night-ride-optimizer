# Preparation handbook: slide explanations (content draft v0.4, 23 Oct 2026)

This handbook backs every slide of [BlueJay_Midterm_v0.4.pptx](BlueJay_Midterm_v0.4.pptx). For each slide it gives:
- the evidence, assumptions and design choices behind it;
- likely questions with defensible answers;
- links to the sections teammates should read.

The context paragraph and the exact narration are in [speaking_script.md](speaking_script.md) under the same anchor (`#s01`–`#s28`).

**Statement labels.**
- **Observed:** read from April's dashboards.
- **Derived:** computed from observed numbers with a stated formula.
- **Assumed:** our modelling choice.
- **Designed:** a hand-built test case or a setting we chose.
- **Simulated:** produced by our model.

**Execution status** is a separate label: *tested in simulation*, *planned — not yet run*, *proposed — not implemented*. "Tested in simulation" never means validated against Hopkins operations.

## 5 · We narrowed the question to one controllable decision
[Script](speaking_script.md#s05)

**Design choice and its justification** (technical review B).
- **We hold one specified dispatcher fixed** so that differences between runs come from the transfer rule.
- **Synthetic nights do not forbid comparing dispatch algorithms.** The same objection, that results depend on our assumptions, applies to rule comparisons too. Our scope reflects three things: the question we chose, the unknown internals of Hopkins' dispatcher, and the need for a controlled comparison.
- **Replanning cadence.** TransLoc's 2016 vendor post describes re-solving at each new ride, cancellation, driver-reported no-show and every 2 minutes. Our van dispatcher is modelled on that design (D-25). Its triggers differ slightly: a van coming on duty triggers a decision moment, while Lyft events do not. The current TransLoc version and any Hopkins dispatcher overrides are unknown ([TransLoc notes §3.2](../../../technical/transloc_operations.md#32-the-scheduling-engine)).
- **The dispatcher is provisional** (D-51), pending a five-van speed check.

**Likely questions.**
- **Why not compare dispatch algorithms, as proposed?** We could, but it would be a different question. We chose the transfer decision because it is one controllable lever, and holding the dispatcher fixed isolates it.
- **Is your dispatcher Hopkins' dispatcher?** No. It is modelled on TransLoc's published 2016 design; Hopkins' current practice is not verified.

## 9 · Knowing the endpoints does not identify the journeys
[Script](speaking_script.md#s09)

**Method** (D-18).
1. **Gravity prior:** w_od = O_o · D_d · e^(−β t_od), where t_od is driving minutes and β = 0.1 per minute, so the weight falls by about ×0.905 per minute. Pairs under 60 s of driving are excluded.
2. **Iterative proportional fitting:** rescale rows and columns in turn until both April location totals match. The largest error is about 10⁻¹⁰.

**Assumptions.**
- β is a choice: every β reproduces the totals, so the data cannot choose it.
- Requests share the geography of completed rides.
- Times are uniform within an hour.
- The same geography holds every hour.

**Not yet tested.** A spread-out geography variant exists but has not been run.

**Likely question.**
- **Why prefer nearby trips?** It is the standard transport-planning prior. The alternatives (β = 0, or 0.2) are sensitivity options.

**Read:** [design trail §5](../../../technical/decision_log.md#5-which-origin-goes-with-which-destination) · [spec §3.3](../../../technical/scenario_spec.md#33-geography-party-size-latent-draws).

## 11 · One status label hides different events
[Script](speaking_script.md#s11)

**Three layers.**
- **Observed:** 23,843 of 50,297 (47.4%) ended "Canceled". The digitized sums also show 99 "Denied".
- **Assumed status mapping:** Lyft hand-offs close as "Canceled" (methodology Appendix C). Not confirmed.

- **Hidden patience:** a Weibull distribution with shape 1.5 and median 18 min, so scale = 18 / (ln 2)^(1/1.5) ≈ 23.0 min. Shape 1.5 means giving up becomes likelier the longer someone has waited.

<a id="s15"></a>
## 15 · The same decision cycle repeats as the evening unfolds
[Script](speaking_script.md#s15)

**Verified implementation order** (`engine.py:305-351`; technical review).
1. Same-second events are processed first, in fixed classes; patience checks come before the decision moment.
2. The solver generates candidates and the MILP picks one route per van.
3. The unassigned timer is updated (and again after any re-solve).
4. The rule reads each request still waiting for a van.
5. Declines are recorded. Transfers leave van planning, with the Lyft ordered at now + 2 min.
6. If at least one transfer happened, there is **one** re-solve, with no second rule pass.
7. Departures; a van commits to its next stop.

**Triggers in the code.**
- A new request.
- A cancellation or no-show while waiting for a van.
- A van coming on duty.
- The 120-s tick.

A van going off duty and Lyft events do not trigger a decision moment. The spec lists off-duty as a trigger, a small spec/code difference recorded in the technical review. At most one decision moment runs per second.

**Likely question.**
- **Could transfers cascade?** No. One rule pass and at most one re-plan per decision moment.

**Read:** [spec §17.3](../../../technical/scenario_spec.md#173-same-second-event-order-d-46) · [spec §17.4](../../../technical/scenario_spec.md#174-epochs-and-solver-timing) · [model design §5](../../../technical/model_design.md#5-rolling-horizon-details).

<a id="s16"></a>
## 16 · The optimizer compares the consequences of candidate plans
[Script](speaking_script.md#s16)

**Objective.** c = Σ q·φ(W) + 0.5 Σ q·H + 0.1 km (+ Ψ only for an unavoidable existing guardrail excess). φ is piecewise linear:

| Minutes waited | 0–5 | 5–10 | 10–15 | 15–20 | 20+ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Slope (points per passenger-minute) | 1 | 2.5 | 5 | 10 | 20 |

- So φ(5) = 5, φ(10) = 17.5, φ(15) = 42.5 and φ(20) = 92.5.
- The 16th minute of waiting costs 10 points.
- The riding weight 0.5 is below every waiting slope.

**Worked example** (dwell omitted).
- **Route A:** W = 12 and 8, H = 8 and 9. Waiting 27.5 + 2×12.5 = 52.5; riding 0.5×(8+18) = 13.0; driving 1.0. Total **66.5**.
- **Route B:** W = 6 (party) and 16; riding 0.5×(20+5) = 12.5. Total **81.0**.

**Postponement.** π_r = b_r + M. M guarantees that the fewest requests the candidates allow are postponed (B2). It does not guarantee that every request is served.

**Constraints.**
- **Hard:** seats; precedence; one route per van; each request served once or postponed.
- **Guardrail:** max(25, 2×direct + 5) min. No plan may create or worsen an excess, but a no-show wait can still push a ride over: 36 events, 16 distinct requests (B8).

**"Optimal."** HiGHS proves optimality over the candidates generated at that decision moment: every subset of at most 3 new pickups per van, each in its best stop order. It is not optimal over all routes, nor over the evening.

**Likely questions.**
- **Why at most 3 new pickups?** Exact ordering with 4 took minutes per decision in Python.
- **Optimal, given 41 incomplete tie searches?** The main solve was proven optimal at all 13,663 decision moments. Enumeration showed the optimum unique in all 41.

**Read:** [methodology §6](../../../technical/scenario_methodology.md#6-the-dispatchers-objective-one-block-at-a-time) · [model design §3.5](../../../technical/model_design.md#35-route-cost) · [§4.1 φ](../../../technical/model_design.md#41-the-convex-wait-penalty-φ) · [§4.3 guardrail](../../../technical/model_design.md#43-the-ride-guardrail-no-unreasonable-onboard-delays).

## 17 · We changed the transfer decision, not the van dispatcher
[Script](speaking_script.md#s17)

**Code definitions** (`lyft.py:13-30`). τ = now, t_r = request time.

| Rule | Moves the request when |
|---|---|
| Wait ten / fifteen minutes | τ − t_r ≥ 600 / 900 s |
| Unassigned for ten minutes | τ − unassigned_since ≥ 600 s |
| Predicted total wait exceeds fifteen minutes | predicted pickup − t_r > 900 s |
| Lyft arrives sooner | predicted pickup > τ + 120 + 480 + 120 s, i.e. now + 12 min |

**Predicted pickup.** The plan's ETA if the request is assigned, otherwise the tentative ETA. If there is no feasible van, it is infinite and the rule fires.

**Eligibility.** State "waiting for a van": not committed to a van, never having declined.

**Reset example for "Unassigned for ten minutes"** (preparation only).
- A request arrives at 18:00 and is outside every plan. Its timer starts at 18:00.
- At 18:06 it enters a van's plan: the timer resets.
- At 18:08 a re-plan drops it again: the timer restarts at 18:08.
- It becomes eligible only at 18:18, if it stays unplanned that long, even though it has waited 18 minutes.

The timer is updated at decision moments, so "continuous" means at decision-moment granularity.

**Thresholds.**
- Fifteen minutes echoes the published pickup-target wording around April 2026 (wording, not verified operating policy; D-49).
- Ten minutes is an earlier trigger.
- Two minutes is a margin against prediction error.

All six were specified before any run (spec §6–§7) and not changed after seeing results. Pre-registration v2 is still a draft, to be frozen before the five-van experiment, so do not say "pre-registered".

<a id="s19"></a>
## 19 · A cheap postponement price could keep a rider waiting
[Script](speaking_script.md#s19)

**The flaw.** The original price b_r = q·φ(next tick − t_r) assumes a van at the next 2-min tick. At 18:15 a party of five that had waited 15 min was charged 262.5 points, against 435.8 for the earliest physical pickup at 18:19:28.

**Controlled comparison:** the designed evening, vans only, one run per formulation (B5).

| | Median / 90th pct / worst wait (min) | Mean (min) | Extra variables |
|---|---|---|---|
| First version | 17.1 / 31.0 / 44.4 | 18.8 | 0 |
| Adopted | 18.6 / 31.2 / 35.6 | 18.8 | 0 |
| Route-based | 21.1 / 34.3 / 38.6 | 21.8 | up to 9,828 |

**Adopted, provisionally (D-53).** b_r = q·φ(max(next tick, L_r) − t_r), with L_r the earliest any on-duty van can reach the pickup.
- It changes cost coefficients only.
- Computing L_r is |open| × |vans| lookups per decision, which was not separately timed. "No added variables" is not a runtime measurement.

**Limitations.**
- The adopted price is still optimistic when the nearest van is busy, and it does not guarantee oldest-first service.
- On this evening the youngest request waited 35.6 min instead of 9.8, so the median rose.
- The route-based version can err in either direction.

**Likely questions.**
- **Your fix made the median worse?** Slightly. We adopted it because the old price was provably below any service a van could give, not for outcomes on one evening. The 90th percentile also rose slightly (31.0 → 31.2 min).
- **Who paid for the lower worst wait?** One request that had waited little went from 9.8 to 35.6 minutes. The worst case moved; it did not vanish.
- **Why "the one that removes the flaw" rather than the simplest?** The first version is simpler, but it carries the flaw. The adopted one fixes the flaw with no added variables; the route-based one adds up to 9,828.

## 22 · The predictive rules reached more requests promptly—and used more Lyft
[Script](speaking_script.md#s22)

**Results** (means over 30 nights; SD in brackets).

| Rule | Reached ≤ 15 min | Lyft orders / night |
|---|---|---|
| Vans only | 0.477 (0.095) | 0.00 |
| Wait ten minutes | 0.514 (0.082) | 5.93 |
| Wait fifteen minutes | 0.495 (0.090) | 3.10 |
| Unassigned for ten minutes | 0.476 (0.096) | 0.23 |
| Predicted total wait exceeds fifteen minutes | 0.735 (0.074) | 9.33 |
| Lyft arrives sooner | 0.750 (0.075) | 10.47 |

**The metric** is curb arrival within 15 min over all 28 requests. Wait percentiles cover only requests picked up, so they drop those who cancelled.

**The metric favours early transfer.** A request moved under "Wait ten minutes" has waited at least 10 min, then faces the 2-min ordering lag and a Lyft delay with median 8 min. It reaches the curb within 15 min only if the delay is under about 3 min, roughly 2%. Under "Wait fifteen minutes", never. So the waiting-time rules can gain on this measure only by freeing vans. Measures with no threshold give the same ordering: requests picked up, 20.2–20.4 against 16.1–17.2, and the 90th-percentile wait (B6). Sensitivity at 10 and 20 min is not yet run.

**Lyft orders vs transfers.** A transfer cancelled during the ordering lag never becomes an order: 196 − 18 = 178 = 5.93 × 30.

**What the comparison does not isolate.**
- The predictive rules also used more Lyft, so their advantage is not measured at an equal Lyft budget.
- Per Lyft order relative to vans only, they add about 0.73–0.77 requests reached, against about 0.16–0.17 for the waiting-time rules.
- That ratio is descriptive and does not control for budget.

**Likely questions.**
- **Isn't your metric rigged for early transfer?** Partly, as above. The ordering is the same on measures with no threshold.
- **Your predictive rules transfer at 0 s elapsed. Isn't that just sending the burst straight to Lyft?** Largely, yes: they act at the first decision moment at which the plan already predicts a long wait. They differ from the waiting-time rules in *when* they move a rider, not in whether they do.
- **Do the pilot nights resemble April?** Only loosely. Under vans only, the 90th-percentile wait among requests picked up is 20.2 min, against April's 17–21 min for completed rides. That is face plausibility, not validation, and our percentiles exclude cancellations.
- **Is the prediction rule better?** It reaches more requests and uses more Lyft. "Better" needs agreed criteria (D-50).
- **Why did "Unassigned for ten minutes" do almost nothing?** It was identical to vans only on 24 of 30 nights, with 8 transfers on 6 nights, all of requests made during the burst (moved at 18:35–18:38). Requests were usually in some van's plan within minutes even when pickup was far off: assignment is not pickup.

## 24 · Better service and lower Lyft use can point to different choices
[Script](speaking_script.md#s24)

**Definitions.**
- **Service:** S = share reached ≤ 15 min (higher is better).
- **Lyft use:** B = Lyft orders per night (lower is better).
- **Dominance:** P dominates Q if S_P ≥ S_Q and B_P ≤ B_Q, with at least one strict.
- **Ties:** identical points do not dominate each other.

**Results.**
- **Five of six are nondominated on the 30-night means.** No noise margin is applied.
- **"Unassigned for ten minutes" is dominated by vans only:** 0.476 against 0.477 service, and 0.23 against 0 Lyft orders. It is identical to vans only on 24 nights; on the other 6 it reached more on 2 and fewer on 2. No statistical margin is applied (D-50).
- **Vans only is nondominated through zero Lyft use alone.**

**Supported and unsupported points.** Minimising −S + λB for any λ ≥ 0 picks only vans only, "Predicted total wait…" or "Lyft arrives sooner". The two waiting-time rules lie below the line from vans only to "Predicted total wait…" (0.477 + 0.0276 B), so no weighting selects them.

**Night by night** (B7). The prediction rules reach more than vans only on all 30 nights; "Wait ten minutes" on 19, and fewer on 6.

**Three trade-offs kept separate.**
1. The routing weights inside the optimizer.
2. This policy comparison.
3. The decision criteria, not yet agreed.

**Likely question.**
- **Is the Pareto set robust to noise?** Not established. Per night, the predictive rules are nondominated on 24 and 12 of 30 nights (B7).
- **Why Lyft orders, not cost?** No Lyft cost is known (D-45). Orders count a regular Lyft and an XL equally, so a cost axis could change the dominance.
- **Is this the Pareto frontier?** No: it is the nondominated set among six tested rules. A frontier would come from threshold sweeps, and a frontier over all policies from the extension on slide 25.

<a id="s25"></a>
## 25 · Could we choose the transfer policy systematically?
[Script](speaking_script.md#s25)

**Corrected framing** (technical review C).
- **A threshold need not be a leader–follower problem.** It can be tuned by simulation-based search, with the dispatcher as a black box; a bilevel formulation is one modelling choice.
- **A finite search can solve a finite problem.** If the follower is defined as our deterministic per-decision restricted MILP with its tie rule, exhaustive search over a finite threshold set finds the best option for the defined objective, averaged over the sampled nights. It does not give optimality over continuous thresholds or all policies.

**One precise version.** min over θ of E[L(θ, y) + λ·B(θ)], subject to y being the follower's response. To make it precise, define:
1. **The leader's space:** rule family, a discrete or continuous threshold, bounds.
2. **The follower:** a sequence of per-decision restricted MILPs whose state depends on earlier decisions and random outcomes, or one whole-evening problem.
3. **Non-unique follower optima:** optimistic or pessimistic (Colson, Marcotte & Savard 2007, *Ann. Oper. Res.* 153:235–256; Dempe 2002). Ours uses a fixed canonical tie rule (D-52), which is neither.
4. **The expectation:** a scenario distribution, or a sample average over N nights.
5. **The loss L and the weight λ** (D-50 open).
6. **Upper-level constraints**, such as a Lyft budget or a service floor.

**Likely questions.**
- **Isn't your loop already leader–follower?** No. The rule reads the current plan, then the optimizer re-plans once. Nothing chooses the rule by anticipating the response.
- **Riders and Lyft as players?** Not modelled as optimizing.
- **Why bilevel and not just simulation optimisation?** It becomes bilevel only if we model the dispatcher as optimizing its own routing points, which differ from the designer's service and Lyft criteria. Simulation-based search treating the dispatcher as a black box is a valid alternative.
- **Is your follower optimistic or pessimistic?** Neither. A fixed canonical tie rule (D-52) makes its response unique. In 41 cases the search for equally good plans was incomplete; in all 41 the optimum was shown unique (B8).

## 26 · Which assumptions could change the answer?

**Priority and reasons.**
